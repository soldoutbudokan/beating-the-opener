Can a model beat the NBA closing line?

A win-probability model built from twelve seasons of games, box scores and player rotations, tested against the Vegas closing price on 3,962 held-out games. The honest answer is no — and the way the first version appeared to win is the most useful thing here.

Held out: 2023-24 · 2024-25 · 2025-26 15,499 games Line-blind, walk-forward
Verdict

The closing line wins on every market tested. The best line-blind model reaches 0.5953 log loss against the closing line's 0.5818, and an encompassing regression finds the model adds no information the price does not already contain (coefficient −0.08, p = 0.43). An earlier version beat the line and returned +12% simulated ROI. That result was a leak, not an edge. Three separate upper bounds — perfect use of every feature, oracle player ratings fit on the future, and an architecture sweep — all land short of the line, so this is a property of the market rather than of the modelling.

01 The bar

What the market actually achieves, before any model exists

De-vigged closing moneylines were recovered for 99.8% of games. The overround averages 4.3%, and four different vig-removal methods (multiplicative, additive, Shin, power) agree to within 0.0005 log loss — so the benchmark is not an artifact of how the juice was stripped out.

02 What the model sees

Two information tiers, deliberately separated

Features are built strictly point-in-time: every rolling statistic updates only after a game's row has been written. On top of Elo and schedule effects sit possession-based efficiency ratings, opponent-adjusted ridge ratings refit weekly, and a minutes-weighted RAPM fit on 1,678 players.

The tiers matter because one class of feature — who is dressed tonight — is known publicly about 30 minutes before tip, and is therefore priced into a closing line but not available a day earlier.

  • Tier A (strict) — 63 features. Nothing from tonight's game at all: prior results, ratings, rest, travel, form.
  • Tier B (pre-tip) — 94 features. Adds which established rotation players are available, plus a lineup-adjusted RAPM.

Models are refit walk-forward: predictions for a season come only from seasons before it. Stack weights and blend weights are fit on 2019–2023 and frozen. The three held-out seasons are scored once.

04 The false positive

How the first version "beat" the market by 0.006 and returned +12% ROI

The first availability features asked a natural question: which players appear in tonight's box score? On held-out data that model tied the closing line, its blend beat the line at p = 0.003, and betting it at real closing prices returned +12.3% ROI across 1,835 wagers, positive in every season.

Nothing about that is plausible. Edges against closing lines are close to zero; a persistent 12% would be one of the most valuable models in sports. So it was treated as a bug report rather than a finding.

The leak

Blowouts empty the bench. In games decided by 20+ points, teams used 24.3 players between them; in games inside 5 points, 19.4. "Who appeared in the box score" is therefore partly a readout of the final margin — and the winning team empties its bench more, so it leaked direction too.

After the fix the apparent edge vanished: Tier B moved from 0.5843 to 0.5953, the blend's advantage disappeared, and no betting threshold was profitable. A leakage guard now runs on every build and fails any feature correlating above 0.12 with the absolute margin.

05 Why the line wins

Two tests that close the question

Encompassing regression. Fit logit(y) ~ logit(market) + logit(model) on the held-out games. If the model knows anything the price does not, its coefficient is positive and significant. It is neither — −0.08 (p = 0.43) for Tier A, −0.11 (p = 0.40) for Tier B, while the market coefficient sits near 1.1 with z ≈ 10–13. The price encompasses the model completely.

Subset search. Fourteen regimes where a market might plausibly be soft — early season, playoffs, back-to-backs, heavy injuries, big favourites, coin flips, rest and travel mismatches. The model beat the line in zero of them. In the heavy-injury quartile, the supposed home-field advantage of an availability model, the market scored 0.5518 against the model's 0.5699.

One last angle: the held-out encompassing fit implied the de-vigged price was slightly under-confident, which would mean a simple sharpening beats it. Fit honestly on validation seasons the correction points the other way (b = 0.95 vs 1.13), and applying it out-of-sample makes the forecast worse. That apparent bias was noise.

06 Three ceilings

Not "we failed to find a win" — bounds showing there isn't one to find

A negative result is only worth as much as the effort behind it. Each remaining lever was therefore bounded rather than abandoned.

Ceiling 1 — better modelling of these features. Project the closing line's logit onto the model's; the residual is the market's private information, and it is strongly predictive (coefficient +1.16, z = 9.97). All 95 observables jointly explain just 17.6% of it. Perfectly exploiting every feature in hand — an optimistic in-sample bound — reaches 0.5932. Still short of 0.5818.

Ceiling 2 — better player ratings. Rather than reconstruct lineups from play-by-play, RAPM was refit on the entire dataset including the held-out seasons, giving each player a rating no causal system could beat because it is fit on the future. It ranks sensibly — Curry, Green, Leonard, Tatum, Durant. The oracle model scores 0.5915, still losing by 0.0097 (p = 0.0001). Perfect player valuation buys 0.0038 of the 0.0135 required, so stint-level RAPM cannot win.

Ceiling 3 — capacity. The hyperparameter sweep preferred heavier regularisation (C = 0.003); a neural network did worse; the same ~0.014 gap reproduces on the validation seasons.

The one input that would matter is a point-in-time injury report — probable/questionable designations and warm-up scratches are what the closing line is reacting to. It is not retrievable: ESPN's injury block returns today's status regardless of the game requested, and the per-athlete history endpoint is gone. Availability alone was worth 0.011 of the Tier A deficit, which is a fair indication of what a real feed would be worth.

So the honest conclusion is not that this model is poor. It lands within 2% of the closing line's log loss, and beats the opening line's accuracy on a strict day-before information set. It is that the closing price aggregates every sharp model plus real-time news plus the information in the order flow itself — and the last two are not in any public post-game dataset.

Data: ESPN results, box scores and odds, 2014-15 through 2025-26 · 15,499 games · 400,559 player-game records. Metrics are log loss and Brier score on de-vigged closing moneylines; significance from a 10,000-sample paired bootstrap on per-game log-loss differences.
